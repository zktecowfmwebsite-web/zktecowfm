Time & Labor Data

Better Payroll Starts at the Punch: How to Improve Time & Labor Data Before It Reaches Workday

Payroll cannot fix source data it never received correctly. The strongest workforce-data strategies prevent avoidable errors at the employee interaction—before they become manager edits, payroll exceptions, costing corrections and support tickets.

ZKTeco WFM InsightsEducational PerspectiveUpdated September 2026

Payroll is often where bad workforce data becomes visible. It is rarely where the problem began. A missing punch, incorrect labor choice, duplicate transaction, wrong department transfer or unrecorded attestation usually originates much earlier—at the moment an employee interacts with the collection system.

Payroll accuracy begins with workforce-data quality

Every downstream process depends on the quality of the event captured upstream. If the employee is identified incorrectly, the timestamp is incomplete, the wrong labor context is attached, or a required response is missing, the organization has created an exception that someone must eventually resolve.

The better question is not simply, “How efficiently can payroll clean up exceptions?” It is: “Which preventable exceptions can we stop or clarify while the employee still has the context to answer?”

Operating example

One wrong labor choice can touch four teams

An employee works six hours on Job A and two hours on Job B, but never records the transfer. The timecard still contains eight valid hours, so the problem may not look like a timekeeping issue. Later, a supervisor notices the labor distribution is wrong, payroll receives a correction, finance gets revised costing and a support question may be opened to determine why the transfer was missed.

At the point of work, the correction might have required a few seconds. Downstream, it can become a multi-person process.

What should be controlled at the source?

Not every payroll rule belongs on a clock. The goal is not to recreate payroll at the workforce edge. The goal is to place simple, understandable controls where they prevent predictable data-quality problems.

Identity

Confirm that the transaction belongs to the correct employee through the credential or authentication method appropriate to the workforce.

Sequence

Help prevent obvious transaction errors such as rapid duplicate punches or invalid punch sequences.

Labor context

Present the job, department, cost center or other labor choice required to make the time meaningful downstream.

Context and acknowledgment

Use targeted prompts or attestations when the organization needs information the system cannot determine automatically.

Use defaults when the system already knows the answer

Employees should not be asked to make accounting decisions unnecessarily. If a worker normally belongs to one department or job, the collection experience can often default that value and ask the employee only when an exception occurs.

This matters because every additional choice creates another opportunity for error. Workday itself supports defaulting and restricting worktags in time entry, which reinforces the broader design principle: present only the labor context needed for the transaction.

What most buyers overlook

Data quality is an employee-experience issue

Organizations sometimes treat controls and usability as opposites. They do not have to be. The best source controls make the correct action easier: fewer irrelevant choices, immediate feedback, clear instructions and a defined path for legitimate exceptions.

A control that blocks valid work or confuses employees can create more corrections than it prevents.

Measure the exceptions you are trying to prevent

Transaction volume alone tells little about quality. A more useful operating dashboard tracks recurring sources of rework, such as:

  • missing punches;
  • duplicate events;
  • manager timecard edits;
  • wrong or missing labor allocations;
  • rejected transactions;
  • frequent overrides;
  • site-specific exception rates.

If one location consistently generates more corrections than another, the root cause may be workflow design, device placement, training, configuration or data synchronization—not employee behavior.

Prevent, warn or allow?

ApproachUse when
PreventThe action is clearly invalid and blocking it will not stop legitimate work.
WarnThe transaction may be unusual but could be legitimate; provide context and allow an authorized path forward.
Capture + flagThe employee must be allowed to work, while the exception is routed for downstream review.

Questions leaders should ask

  • Which time and labor exceptions consume the most manager or payroll effort?
  • Which of those errors are predictable at the point of collection?
  • Can the system determine the correct value automatically?
  • Which rules should block versus warn?
  • How does the employee proceed when the exception is legitimate?
  • Can we measure exception rates by site, clock or workflow?
  • Can support trace an event from the employee interaction through Workday?
Decision rule
Move preventable errors upstream, but keep legitimate work moving.

Use the workforce edge to improve identity, sequence, labor context and immediate feedback. Keep complex payroll calculations and policy ownership in the appropriate system of record.

Key takeaway

Payroll cleanup is often a symptom of a problem that began much earlier. The strongest time-and-labor architecture captures the actual event, gives the employee enough context to get it right, prevents obvious errors where appropriate and preserves a clear path for legitimate exceptions. Better payroll data therefore begins at the workforce edge—not in the final payroll review. When the source event is cleaner, every downstream process has a better starting point.
